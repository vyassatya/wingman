THE GYM BUSINESS PREMIUM BOUTIQUE ECONOMICS PLAYBOOK

Unit economics, cost structure, break-even and payback for a premium boutique gym in Bangalore. Benchmarks: Planet Fitness FY25 Annual Report, Life Time FY25, Cult.fit FY25 (RoC filings). Boutique P&L; is a constructed model based on stated assumptions. June 2026.

This playbook breaks down how a premium boutique gym actually makes money. The surrounding market and benchmark figures are drawn from listed and disclosed operators. The boutique profit and loss model itself is constructed from industry-standard assumptions, clearly stated, because a single private boutique does not publish audited unit economics. Treat the model as a well-reasoned planning framework, not audited fact, and adjust every input to your own site.

Where a boutique sits This end of the spectrum

The single most important insight in the gym business: the low-price model is mathematically built on members NOT showing up. Planet Fitness holds 6,000 to 10,000+ members per club where the average member visits only 4 to 6 times a month. A premium boutique is the opposite bet entirely: far fewer members, each paying much more, each expected to show up often. You are not selling cheap access to a crowd. You are selling a high-touch result to a few. That single choice drives every number in the rest of this playbook.

*Life Time average revenue per membership is an annualised centre figure, not directly comparable to Planet Fitness monthly dues. Shown to illustrate the model gap.

Personal training is the real margin engine, not memberships. In this model memberships are 58% of revenue but carry the full fixed cost of the space. PT is 30% of revenue on far higher margin because it is sold per session against a trainer cost. Boutiques that survive push PT attach rate hard: the membership gets the person through the door, PT is where the money is made. A boutique running on memberships alone rarely clears break-even.

The two costs that quietly kill gyms are salaries and electricity. Together they can eat 30 to 38% of revenue. Air conditioning a large space plus running equipment gives gyms one of the highest utility loads of any retail business. A boutique that under-prices memberships to fill the floor often finds these two fixed costs turn an apparently full gym into a loss-making one. Margin lives in pricing power and PT mix, not in cramming in more low-paying members.

The honest verdict: a premium boutique gym is a real business, not a quick one. Break-even needs roughly 60-65% of target membership, which typically takes 9 to 15 months to reach. Payback on capex runs 4 to 6 years at healthy occupancy. This is a patient, operations-heavy business where the winners compete on member results, retention, and PT attach, not on discounting memberships. Anyone promising faster payback is usually under-counting salaries, electricity, or the ramp time to fill the floor.

Five Levers That Decide Whether a Boutique Gym Survives

One. Personal training attach rate. Memberships pay the rent. PT pays the profit. Every point of PT attach rate moves the P&L; more than ten new low-ticket members.

Two. Retention over acquisition. A boutique lives on members who stay 18-24 months, not on a churn-and-burn sign-up machine. Retention is cheaper than acquisition and it compounds.

Three. Pricing discipline. The instinct to discount to fill the floor is what quietly kills premium gyms. Salaries and electricity are fixed. Under-pricing memberships just fills the room with members who do not cover cost.

Four. Location and catchment. A premium boutique needs a dense, high-income catchment within a short drive. The wrong location cannot be fixed by good training.

Five. Trainer quality and stability. Members follow trainers. High trainer churn drives member churn directly. Retaining good trainers is a retention strategy, not just an HR one.
