Theobroma

How a single Colaba bakery became a Rs 2,678 crore company in 21 years

For operators and founders in food and beverage retail | August 2026 | Sources: MCA filings, Business Standard, Business Today, company disclosures

The short version

In October 2004, two sisters opened a four-table bakery in a converted doctor's clinic in Colaba, using Rs 1 crore from their father. In July 2025, ChrysCapital agreed to buy roughly 90 percent of it for Rs 2,410 crore, valuing the company at about Rs 2,678 crore.

The number that matters to a single-unit operator: at 250 stores, the buyer valued each Theobroma outlet at roughly Rs 10.7 crore, against annual revenue of about Rs 2.6 crore per store. Net profit in the final year was only Rs 22.5 crore, and falling. Nobody was paying for profit. They were paying for a repeatable unit.

How it started

Kainaz Messman was 24 and a pastry chef at The Oberoi Mumbai when a bulging disc ended her ability to stand in a professional kitchen. She refused a desk job.

Her mother, Kamal, had run a home-based cake business for years. Her father, Farokh, put up Rs 1 crore on one condition: it would never be repaid. Her sister, Tina Messman Wykes, returned from London to build it with her. They opened on Dussehra 2004 with four small tables, using their grandparents' flat next door as the kitchen. The break-even target was Rs 22,000 a day. They beat it in the first week. The brownie and the mawa cake, still the two largest sellers, came from their mother's recipes.

What the numbers looked like

Revenue grew nearly fivefold in four years. Profit did not follow: net profit fell 19 percent in FY25 as cost growth of 29 percent outpaced revenue growth of 27 percent.

Why it was worth this much

  • They took outside capital before they needed it. Rs 130 crore arrived in 2017 at roughly 30 stores, and went into central kitchens and supply chain rather than storefronts. It bought the ability to open the next hundred stores, not the next ten.

  • They built the kitchen before the store. The rule was to get the commissary kitchen and product specs right before entering a new city. A single-unit owner optimises the store. Theobroma optimised what makes the next fifty stores possible.

  • They productised the craft. The same 2004 recipes, produced to spec at scale. A packageable product is inventory, and inventory is valuable to a buyer. Chef-dependent craft is not.

  • They were valued on the multiple, not the margin. Rs 22.5 crore of profit produced a Rs 2,410 crore cheque. Single stores are valued on what they earn. Systems are valued on what they can be copied into.

The takeaway. A Theobroma store does about Rs 2.5 crore a year. That is a good cafe, not an extraordinary one. What made each one worth Rs 10 crore is that there were 250 of them running the same playbook. The question is not whether a store is profitable. It is whether it is repeatable. Profit produces an income. Repeatability produces an exit.
