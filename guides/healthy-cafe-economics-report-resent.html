STRATEGY NOTE

Healthy Cafe Economics

What a Subway India reference case reveals about the economics of building a healthy-food QSR

Executive summary: the format works when four conditions are simultaneously true
Metric Reference Note
Annual store sales ₹1.1-1.3 Cr ₹30-35k/day reference
Gross margin 73-75% strong product economics
Store capex ₹70-75L current build reference
Store EBITDA 13-15% double-digit operating profit

  1. The format is capital-efficient, not sales-maximizing. A Subway-sized format generates materially lower absolute revenue than McDonald's or KFC, but compensates with compact stores, lower staffing and lower upfront capital. This makes the model easier to replicate across more locations.

  2. Gross margin is not the bottleneck. A 73-75% gross margin is healthy enough to support the concept. The real swing factors are rent, manpower, channel mix and capital discipline. A store with strong gross margin can still produce weak EBITDA if these four items are poorly managed.

  3. A ₹1Cr+ store is easier to visualize when translated into transactions. At an average ticket of roughly ₹325, ₹32,500 of daily sales is approximately 100 transactions per day. This is the most useful demand test before signing a lease.

  4. Delivery can materially reshape the P&L.; With more than half of sales coming from delivery in the reference case, aggregator commissions alone can absorb a meaningful share of store EBITDA. A healthy-food concept should therefore design dine-in, takeaway and direct ordering as economic channels, not just customer conveniences.

Bottom line

A healthy cafe can be an attractive operating business, but the winning model is not 'premium food + beautiful interiors'. It is a disciplined unit-economics system: enough daily transactions, high gross margin, controlled capex, lean staffing and a channel mix that preserves contribution margin.

3 | CAPITAL ARCHITECTURE

The biggest capex lesson: equipment matters more than interiors

A new-store build in the reference case lands around ₹70-75 lakh depending on region. The cost is not predominantly decorative fit-out; commercial equipment, power backup and operating infrastructure account for the majority of the budget.

What this changes in a cafe build

The correct order is menu -> equipment list -> kitchen workflow -> utilities -> customer area -> aesthetics. Reversing that sequence often creates unnecessary civil work, oversized kitchens or expensive equipment that the menu does not need.

Use two capex benchmarks, not one

₹55-60L is a useful 'high-efficiency' industry benchmark; ₹70-75L is a more conservative current-build reference. Underwrite the business on the latter and treat any savings as upside rather than making the business case depend on unusually low capex.

4 | CHANNEL ECONOMICS

Delivery is a growth channel - but it can also be the largest hidden EBITDA leak

The reference network remains delivery-heavy, although dine-in share improved in FY2026. This matters because delivery orders carry aggregator commissions, packaging and promotional funding that do not apply to dine-in transactions.

Three implications for a new healthy-food format

1. Price delivery correctly

Do not treat delivery as the same product sold through another channel. The channel has a different contribution margin and should be priced, promoted and packaged accordingly.

2. Design for takeaway and direct ordering from day one

Every percentage point of demand shifted from high-commission delivery toward takeaway, direct digital ordering or dine-in can improve store economics without changing food cost.

3. Track contribution margin by channel - not only total sales

A store can grow GMV while EBITDA deteriorates. The operating dashboard should separate dine-in, takeaway, direct delivery and aggregator delivery economics.

5 | FOUNDER PLAYBOOK

The benchmark is most useful as a decision framework before a lease is signed

For a founder building a healthy-food concept, the key is to translate the benchmark into a small number of non-negotiable thresholds. These are not targets to copy blindly; they are questions the concept must answer convincingly.

Decision question Reference threshold Founder interpretation
Can the catchment support demand? ~₹30-35k/day Reverse-engineer into bills/day before choosing the site
Is the average ticket credible? ₹300-350 Price architecture must match the local customer
Can food cost stay controlled? 26-28% COGS Premium ingredients cannot be allowed to erase gross margin
Can the store run lean? 8-10 staff Menu complexity should not require restaurant-level manpower
Is capex disciplined? ~₹70-75L Set a ceiling before design development begins
Does the store create operating profit? 13-15% EBITDA Revenue growth without EBITDA is not the goal
Is delivery dependence survivable? Stress-test 10-18% fee Model channel contribution, not only top-line sales

Five high-value conclusions

1. Underwrite on ₹70L+ capex; optimize toward ₹55-60L

This keeps the business case conservative. If the model only works at the lowest possible build cost, the risk is already too high.

2. Think in transactions, not crores

A ₹1.19Cr store at a ₹325 ticket is roughly 100 bills/day. That is a far more actionable demand question than asking whether a location can 'do ₹1Cr'.

3. Standardize the backend; customize the customer experience

The economic power of a healthy-food concept comes from a limited set of standardized ingredients, portions and processes that can create many customer-facing combinations without creating kitchen complexity.

4. Beverages are a potential ticket-size lever

The reference format has a relatively low beverage contribution versus McDonald's and KFC. A healthy cafe with functional drinks, smoothies, protein beverages or coffee may have room to increase mix and average ticket if beverage margins are attractive.

5. The best site is the site that passes the unit-economics test

Visibility and aesthetics matter only after the rent-to-sales equation works. Demand, lease economics and capex should be solved before emotional attachment to a location.

METHODOLOGY & NOTES

Definitions, derivations and source notes

All analytical conclusions in this report are based on the operating, financial and industry data disclosed in the EverBrands India Draft Red Herring Prospectus dated 28 September 2026, including the embedded industry report prepared by The Knowledge Company (TKC).

How the numbers have been treated

Category Treatment in this report
Company operating data COCO store count, average daily sales, gross margin, delivery/dine-in mix, historical capex per opened store, average store size and rollout data are used as observed operating references.
Industry benchmarks Peer APC, gross margin, store EBITDA, capex, staffing, store size and sales/day are used as market benchmarks, not as audited company-store P&Ls.;
Derived metrics Annualized sales, midpoint EBITDA, rough capex/EBITDA, implied transactions/day, capex-per-sq-ft and delivery-commission sensitivities are arithmetic derivations from the disclosed inputs.
Capex convention The report uses ~₹70-75L as the primary planning reference because it aligns with recent average COCO store capex and forward new-store estimates. ₹55-60L is retained as a high-efficiency industry comparison.

Important limitations

This report is an educational business-analysis summary. It is not a franchise offer, investment recommendation, valuation opinion or forecast of store performance. Actual economics can vary materially by city, rent, property type, menu, pricing, sourcing, staffing, equipment specification, taxes, financing, working capital, maintenance capex, store maturity and delivery mix. Peer benchmarks may use different accounting conventions and may not be directly comparable. Simple capex/EBITDA ratios shown in this report are not IRRs or accounting payback periods and exclude financing, taxation, working capital, maintenance capex and lease-accounting effects.
